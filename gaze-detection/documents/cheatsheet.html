Reference sheet · webcam eye tracking

Gaze Detection: every part explained

How a plain 720p laptop webcam works out where someone is looking on screen — no infrared, no depth sensor, no headset. Every term, feature and number in the project, in one place.

01 The idea in one minute

A camera can see which way your eyes point, but not where on the screen that lands — the same eye angle means a different spot depending on how far away and how high your head sits. So the system does three things: measure the eyes, lock the head into a known position, and learn the personal mapping from "eye angle" to "screen pixel".

01
Camera captures a frame
1280×720, mirrored so it behaves like a mirror. Native 16:9 — using 640×480 would crop the lens and break the distance maths.
02
MediaPipe finds 478 face landmarks
Points on the face, including the irises. Everything geometric is derived from these.
03
Positioning gate checks you are sat correctly
45–65 cm away and roughly centred. Out of range, nothing downstream is recorded or trusted.
04
L2CS neural network reads eye direction
Takes a square crop of the face, returns two angles: gaze_pitch and gaze_yaw.
05
Calibration converts angles → screen position
A polynomial learned from your own data. Output is (x, y) between 0 and 1.

02 Pitch, yaw, roll — the three ways a thing rotates

Borrowed from aircraft. Any object rotating in 3D does it around three axes, and these are their names. The project uses them twice: once for the head, once for the eyes.

PITCH
Nodding “yes”
Tilting up / down. Rotation about the side-to-side axis.
YAW
Shaking “no”
Turning left / right. Rotation about the vertical axis.
ROLL
Ear toward shoulder
Tilting sideways. Rotation about the axis pointing at the camera.
The one thing people mix up: head_pitch is which way your head points. gaze_pitch is which way your eyes point. You can hold your head still and move only your eyes — that is why both are recorded separately.

03 Distance — how a flat image knows how far away you are

The known-size trick

pos_distance_cm
What
How far your eyes are from the screen, in centimetres.
Why
Eye angle alone is ambiguous — the same angle hits a different screen point at 45 cm than at 65 cm. Distance is also what the positioning gate enforces.
How
The gap between your two pupils is roughly 6.3 cm for every adult. The camera sees that gap as a number of pixels. Closer = more pixels. Rearranging the camera equation gives distance.
distance_cm = focal_px × 6.3 / ipd_px ipd_px pixels between the two irises (landmarks 468 ↔ 473) focal_px the camera's "zoom factor" in pixels — 1073 on this laptop, measured by sitting at exactly 50 cm and pressing 'c' worked example: 1073 × 6.3 / 130 px = 52 cm
Same idea as a camera-only car. A reversing car with no ultrasonic sensor judges distance the same way — it knows a number plate is 52 cm wide, measures how wide it looks, and works backwards. We know pupils sit 6.3 cm apart.

05 Reading the CSV: fixed points vs your data

Every row pairs what the camera measured with where the dot actually was. Those are the two halves of the file.

The fixed data — the answer

target_x, target_y

Where the dot was drawn on screen, as a fraction: 0,0 is top-left, 1,1 is bottom-right, 0.5,0.5 is dead centre.

  • The program drew the dot, so this is known exactly.
  • Never a guess or a prediction.
  • 2 columns — sits right after the ID columns at the front.
Your data — the measurements

the 32 feature columns

Everything the webcam observed about your face and eyes at the moment you were looking at that dot.

  • Changes person to person, frame to frame.
  • This is what the model reads from.
  • Starts at gaze_pitch and runs to pos_face_dy.
source user session_id target_x target_y ← what + THE ANSWER milestone4_pursuit arsi 20260721-000011 0.076425 0.050000 gaze_pitch gaze_yaw head_pitch ear_left pos_distance_cm … ← YOUR MEASUREMENTS 0.113651 0.064504 -14.288233 0.234893 54.500287
Where the dot was fixed vs moving: in milestone4_pursuit rows the dot glides along a path, so target values change smoothly. In milestone7_game rows it sits still at a random spot until popped. The strictly fixed grids — the 5 validation dots and the 9 test dots — are deliberately never saved, because they are the exam paper: reusing them for training would make every accuracy score a lie.

06 Calibration and why it must be personal

Why calibrate at all

Problem
The network gives an angle, not a pixel. Your face shape, eye spacing, seating height and screen size all change what that angle lands on.
Fix
Show dots at known places, record the angles, fit a curve from angle → screen position for this person.
Result
A degree-3 polynomial with 10 terms, stored in calibration_model.pkl.

Drift correction

Problem
You never sit down in exactly the same pose twice. One or two degrees of head tilt shifts every prediction by the same amount.
Symptom
On the accuracy map, all error arrows point the same way and are the same length.
Fix
Look at one centre dot at the start of a session, measure the offset, subtract it from everything after.
Why the dot sweeps the edges twice. A plain left-right-left snake only touches the screen edges for an instant, so the curve had almost no corner data and had to guess out there — the top-right corner was wrong by 6.2 cm. Adding a slow lap around the perimeter dropped that same corner to 0.7 cm.

07 Aim assist, and how the game trains the model

The game does two jobs beyond being fun: it helps you hit targets (aim assist) and it collects training data while you play, so the model improves the more you use it.

Aim assist — the hidden magnet

How the pull works

milestone7
What
When your gaze cursor gets near a target it is dragged toward it — gently at the edge, locking on completely when close. Like aim assist in a console shooter.
Why
Eye tracking is accurate to about 2 cm. Without help, small targets are frustrating to hit even when you are looking straight at them.
How
assisted = raw + (target − raw) × pull, where pull grows from 0 at the edge of the magnet field to 1.0 inside the snap zone.
Solid inner ring = what you see. Everything outside it is invisible.
ZoneSizeVisible?What happens
Target ring 70 px · 1.9 cm Yes — the circle you see Cursor inside here for 0.8 s pops the target.
Snap zone 130 px · 3.5 cm No Cursor locks fully onto the target centre.
Magnet field 330 px · 8.9 cm No Cursor is dragged inward, stronger the closer it gets.
Outside — — No help. Cursor follows your gaze exactly.
Why it once looked broken. The first version used a 160 px magnet with a maximum pull of 65%. But the tracker's own error is often 150–220 px — so the cursor usually never entered the field at all, and even when it did, a 65% pull could not finish the job. Widening it to 330 px and allowing a full lock is what made it work. Press d during the game to reveal the hidden zones.

The training loop — playing makes it better

Why the game is also a data collector

milestone7_game
What
While you hold your gaze on a target, the program saves what the camera sees along with that target's position — a free labelled training row.
Why
Calibration alone gives one sweep of data. Every game adds more, from more head positions, so the model keeps improving instead of going stale.
How
After each game the rows are appended to the CSV and the model is refitted on everything collected so far (once there are 200+ rows).
The trap this had to avoid. Aim assist puts the cursor on the target even when you are not looking there. If training data were taken from the assisted cursor, the assist would be inventing its own answers and poisoning the dataset. So data is only ever judged from your raw gaze, and only when it is genuinely still — held within 3.2 cm for a quarter second. Steady eyes count, darting eyes do not.
Why “still” and not “close to the target”. If we only saved rows where the gaze already landed near the dot, we would only ever collect examples the model already gets right — and learn nothing. The valuable rows are the ones where your eyes are locked on the target but the model is still wrong. Those are the corrections.
